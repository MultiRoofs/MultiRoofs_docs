# Training path for local authorities

```{admonition} Learning objectives
:class: tip
After reading this page, you will be able to:
- Plan a training on the MultiRoofs approach for staff of a public authority.
- Select the modules, pages, tools and exercises that fit your participants.
- Adapt the training to the local context of the participating authorities.
```

## Who this page is for

This page is for anyone who wants to train staff of public authorities in the MultiRoofs approach: a city that wants to build internal capacity, a regional authority supporting its municipalities, a network of cities, a university or a consultancy. It describes a modular training path built on the content of this site, which you can shorten, extend or reorder to fit your audience.

## What the training should achieve

The aim is to explain what multifunctional rooftops are and help participants **apply the approach in their own organisation**. By the end of the training, participants should be able to:

- explain why multifunctional rooftops matter for their city;
- identify and prioritise the urban challenges in their city and districts;
- assess what their building stock and roofs can offer;
- understand the motivations of building owners and the incentives that can influence them;
- follow the 8-step rooftop activation roadmap from priorities to action;
- understand what digital twins and the MultiRoofs tools can and cannot tell them;
- identify the first steps to take in their own organisation.

Teach the approach **as a whole**. Training on isolated topics, such as green roofs or subsidies, misses its main value: connecting needs, capacities and desires.

## Before you start

### Know your participants

Rooftop activation involves several departments. A good training group mixes profiles, so that participants learn to speak a shared language:

- urban planners and policy officers (climate, energy, water, biodiversity, housing, mobility);
- programme and project managers;
- asset and real estate managers;
- data and GIS specialists.

Before the training, find out for each participating authority:

- which roles will attend, and their familiarity with GIS and 3D data;
- which urban challenges are on the political agenda;
- which data is available (see [Module 6](#module-6-digital-twins-and-data));
- which rooftop policies or incentives already exist;
- which language participants work in.

### Prepare local material

The training works best when participants work on their own city. Ask each authority to bring, where possible:

- a map of its districts;
- its main policy documents on climate, energy, housing or green space;
- a list of existing incentives related to buildings or roofs;
- examples of rooftop projects in the city, if any.

### Module 1: Why multifunctional rooftops

**Goal.** Understand the problem MultiRoofs addresses and the logic of the approach.

**Key content.** Pressure on urban space; rooftops as an underused resource; the seven rooftop colours at a glance; golden combinations; why activation is a systemic, not a technical, problem; Need, Can and Want.

**Read.** [About MultiRoofs](../about-multiroofs/index.md), [Rooftop functions](../rooftops-and-buildings/rooftop-functions.md).

```{admonition} Example for audience interactions 
:class: note

**Facilitator:** "Think of a roof you see from your office window, or pass every day. What could it do for the city?"

**Participant A:** "The flat roof of the primary school next to our office. The neighbourhood has almost no green space, and the schoolyard is too small. It could be a green and red roof: a garden that pupils use during the day and residents in the evening."

**Participant B:** "The supermarket roof in the industrial zone. It is huge and empty. It could produce solar energy and store rainwater, so a yellow and blue roof. The area floods after heavy rain."

**Facilitator follow-up:** "What would stop these ideas from happening tomorrow?" Typical answers (who owns the roof, whether it can carry the weight, who pays, who is responsible for safety and access) lead naturally to the Need, Can and Want perspectives.
```

### Module 2: Need, urban challenges

**Goal.** Identify, prioritise and quantify the urban challenges of a city and its districts.

**Key content.** The ten urban challenges and their sub-challenges; adapting them to the local context; differences between districts; turning challenges into measurable targets (roadmap steps 1 to 3).

**Read.** [Urban challenges](../urban-challenges/index.md).

**Tool.** [Urban Challenges assessment](../tools/challenge_assessment/index.md).

```{admonition} Example for audience interactions 
:class: note

**Facilitator:** "Score each challenge from 0 (not relevant) to 3 (top priority), first for the city as a whole, then for two districts."

A group from a fictional mid-sized city produces:

| Urban challenge | City | Historic centre | Industrial zone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Climate adaptation and resilience | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| Loss of nature and biodiversity | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| Decarbonizing energy systems | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| Aging, unfit, or fragile infrastructure | 1 | 1 | 3 |
| Sustainable and equitable mobility | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| Affordable housing | 3 | 2 | 0 |
| Urban health and well-being | 2 | 3 | 1 |

**Facilitator:** "Where do the city and district scores differ, and why?"

**Participant:** "Housing is a top priority for the city, but there is no housing in the industrial zone, so it scores 0 there. Grid capacity is a much bigger issue in the industrial zone than in the city as a whole."

**Facilitator:** "Pick one priority challenge. Does a policy document already set a target for it?"

**Participant:** "Our climate plan aims to retain an extra 50,000 m³ of rainwater by 2035. That gives us a target for blue roofs."
```

### Module 3: Can, rooftops and buildings

**Goal.** Understand what determines the potential of a roof.

**Key content.** The seven-colour system and golden combinations; building characteristics (use, construction era, roof type, height, regulations); urban district categories; the rooftop passport (roadmap steps 4 and 5).

**Read.** [Rooftops and buildings](../rooftops-and-buildings/index.md) and its three subpages.

```{admonition} Example for audience interactions
:class: note

**Facilitator:** "Classify three buildings from your city, then decide which rooftop colours would be feasible."

| Building | Primary use | Construction era | Roof type | District | Feasible colours | Main constraints |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Town hall | Public and institutional | Before 1830 | Pitched | City centre | Yellow (limited) | Heritage protection; no flat surface |
| Social housing block | Residential | 1946–1975 | Flat | Residential (high density) | Green, blue, yellow | Load-bearing capacity must be checked; decision by housing corporation |
| Distribution warehouse | Industrial | After 2000 | Flat | Industrial and services | Yellow, blue, grey | Lightweight steel structure; limited access |

**Facilitator:** "Which of these would you choose for a first project?"

**Participant:** "The social housing block. The roof is flat, the owner is a single professional organisation, and the district lacks green space. But we would need a structural survey first, because buildings from that period often have limited load capacity."

**Facilitator:** "Which golden combination would fit?"

**Participant:** "Green and blue: retaining water to support the vegetation and cool the roof."
```

### Module 4: Want, owners and incentives

**Goal.** Understand owner motivations and how public authorities can influence them.

**Key content.** The ten owner types and their drivers; moments of opportunity; the nine instrument types and six characteristics of incentives; what works in practice; the roles a local authority can take.

**Read.** [Property owners](../owners-and-incentives/property-owners.md), [Incentives](../owners-and-incentives/incentives.md), [Roles of local authorities](../owners-and-incentives/roles-of-local-authorities.md).

```{admonition} Example for audience interactions
:class: note

**Part 1: owner types and moments of opportunity**

**Facilitator:** "Who owns the roofs in your chosen district, and when are they most likely to say yes?"

**Participant:** "In our 19th-century residential district, most buildings belong to private individual homeowners and homeowner associations, plus some small landlords. The best moments are reroofing and energy renovations, which many associations are planning because of new energy requirements."

**Part 2: classifying an existing incentive**

**Facilitator:** "Take one of your incentives and run it through the [decision tree](../owners-and-incentives/incentives.md)."

**Participant:** "Our municipal green roof subsidy pays up to 50% of the cost after the works, until the yearly budget runs out."

- Questions 1 to 8: no. It is not a plan, rule, advice, negotiated agreement, access arrangement, collective finance, loan or tax measure.
- Question 9: yes, it is non-repayable money. **Type: grant.**
- Fixed by the type: financial nature, attached to the project, investment stage.
- Added characteristics: reducing upfront costs, municipal funding, first-come allocation.

**Facilitator:** "Which owner types does it not reach?"

**Participant:** "Homeowner associations rarely apply: they need a collective decision and a design before they can apply, and the subsidy only pays after the works. Advice or a pre-financed grant for the feasibility study might help them."

**Part 3: roles of the local authority**

**Participant:** "Our climate department mainly acts as a policy maker and project manager. Nobody plays the role of public networker: we never bring owners of the same street together to share costs."
```

### Module 5: From priorities to action

**Goal.** Bring needs, capacities and desires together into decisions.

**Key content.** The MultiRoofs framework; the full 8-step roadmap; clarifying priorities, including gaps between city and owner priorities; comparing rooftop options with ground-level alternatives; non-financial constraints as costs; cross-cutting principles.

**Read.** [From priorities to action](../from-priorities-to-action/index.md).

```{admonition} Example for audience interactions
:class: note

**Facilitator:** "Walk through the eight steps for one district."

A group working on a dense 1960s residential district summarises:

| Step | Result |
|---|---|
| 1. City priorities | Climate adaptation, affordable housing, nature and biodiversity |
| 2. District priorities | Heat stress and lack of green space |
| 3. Targets | Reduce the number of hot spots identified in the city's heat map; add 2 ha of accessible green space |
| 4. Owners and buildings | Mostly flat-roofed apartment blocks from 1946–1975; owned by a housing corporation and homeowner associations |
| 5. Rooftop passport | Many roofs are flat and large, but load capacity is unknown |
| 6. Scenarios | The housing corporation's roofs match the heat and green space priorities |
| 7. Combinations | Green and blue for cooling; green and red for a shared roof garden on one pilot block |
| 8. Incentives | Advisory service and a voucher for structural surveys; a negotiated agreement with the housing corporation |

**Facilitator:** "Compare your rooftop solution with a ground-level alternative."

**Participant:** "A new park would provide more green space, but there is no free land in the district. The roofs are the only option for extra green, although they come with costs that are not only financial: structural checks, safety for public access, and maintenance."

**Facilitator:** "Where do the city's priorities and the owners' priorities diverge?"

**Participant:** "We want cooling and biodiversity. The housing corporation cares about maintenance costs and tenant satisfaction. We should present the roof garden as an amenity for tenants, not only as a climate measure."
```

### Module 6: Digital twins and data

**Goal.** Understand what a digital twin is, what data it needs, and what it can and cannot do.

**Key content.** Digital model, shadow and twin; the digital twin cycle; data types and sources; 3D building models and levels of detail; challenges, risks and limitations; data governance and privacy.

**Read.** [Digital twins](../digital-twins/index.md) and its subpages.

```{admonition} Example for audience interactions
:class: note

**Facilitator:** "Check which data your authority has, and under which conditions."

A participant from a fictional municipality fills in:

| Dataset | Available? | Format | Coordinate system | Updated | Licence or access |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Building footprints | Yes | GeoPackage | National grid | Monthly | Open |
| Classified aerial LiDAR | Yes, 2022 | LAZ | National grid | Every 5 years | Open |
| 3D models with roof shapes (LoD2.2) | No | | | | Can be reconstructed from footprints and LiDAR |
| Persistent building identifiers | Partly | | | | Footprints and cadastre use different IDs |
| Heritage protection | Yes | Web map service | National grid | Irregular | Open |
| Flood and heat maps | Yes | GeoTIFF | National grid | 2021 | Regional authority, open |
| Energy labels | Partly | CSV | Addresses only | Continuous | National register |
| Ownership data | Yes | Database | | Continuous | Internal only; personal data under the GDPR |

**Facilitator:** "What does this tell you?"

**Participant:** "We can create 3D models ourselves, so the roof analysis is feasible. The weak point is linking datasets: the identifiers do not match, and energy labels only have addresses. Ownership data is available but sensitive, so we should involve our data protection officer before using it."

**Facilitator:** "Is your city's 3D model a digital model, a digital shadow or a digital twin?"

**Participant:** "A digital model. It is updated every few years when there is a new survey, and nothing flows back to the physical city automatically."
```

### Module 7: Using the MultiRoofs tools

**Goal.** Learn how the tools support each step of the approach, and how to interpret their results.

**Key content.** What each tool does and when to use it; how to prepare data; reading and interpreting outputs; understanding their limitations; using results in internal and external discussions.

**Read.** The tutorials and how-to guides of each tool: [MultiRoofs Builder](../tools/CityJSON/index.md), [MRIO](../tools/mrio/index.md), [Roofy](../tools/roofy/index.md),
[Urban Challenges assessment](../tools/challenge_assessment/index.md).

```{admonition} Example for audience interactions
:class: note

% TODO: replace this example with real tool outputs once the tools are available.

**Facilitator** (showing a map of a district in which roofs are coloured by potential rooftop functions): "The tool shows that 40% of the flat roofs in this district are suitable for blue roofs. What does 'suitable' mean here?"

**Participant (policy):** "That those roofs could hold water, I suppose."

**Participant (data team):** "It means they meet the criteria the tool checks, such as slope and area. It does not mean their structure can carry the weight of the water. That information is not in the data."

**Facilitator:** "What else would you need before contacting the owners?"

**Participants:** "Structural information, heritage restrictions, who owns the roofs, and whether those owners are planning work on their buildings."

**Facilitator:** "How would you present this map to the alderman?"

**Participant:** "As an indication of potential, not as a list of roofs that will be converted. It shows where to focus, and which information we still need to collect."
```

**Tip.** Demonstrate the tools with a familiar example, preferably from one of the participants' cities, and focus on interpretation rather than software operation. Policy staff need to understand what the results mean; data teams need to know how to produce them.

### Module 8: Embedding the approach in your organisation

**Goal.** Translate what was learned into concrete next steps.

**Key content.** Which departments must be involved; who owns the process and the data; how the approach fits existing plans, programmes and renovation cycles; how to start small with a pilot district.

**Read.** [Roles of local authorities](../owners-and-incentives/roles-of-local-authorities.md), [From priorities to action](../from-priorities-to-action/index.md).

```{admonition} Example for audience interactions
:class: note

**Facilitator:** "Draft a one-page action plan for your authority."

An example from a participant:

| Item | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Priority district** | Station district: dense, heat stress, frequent local flooding |
| **Challenges addressed** | Urban heat islands; stormwater management |
| **Target** | Retain an additional 5,000 m³ of rainwater on roofs by 2030 |
| **Data needed** | 3D roof models (to be reconstructed from LiDAR); structural information from the housing corporation |
| **Departments to involve** | Climate adaptation (lead), spatial planning, real estate, GIS, legal (for ownership data) |
| **Owner groups** | Housing corporation; municipal real estate (two schools); homeowner associations |
| **Roles of the authority** | Project manager for municipal roofs; public networker for the homeowner associations |
| **First three steps** | 1. Activate the two school roofs as visible examples. 2. Agree on a joint survey with the housing corporation. 3. Organise an information evening for homeowner associations, with free advice. |

**Facilitator:** "Who in your organisation needs to agree before step 1 can start?"

**Participant:** "The real estate department, which manages the schools, and the alderman for education. We have never worked together on roofs before, so the first step is a meeting between the two departments."
```

## Example programmes

Choose the modules that match your goals.

| Programme | Modules | Suited for |
|---|---|---|
| **Introduction** | 1, 2 (short), 5 (short) | Raising awareness among decision-makers |
| **Methodology** | 1 to 5, and 8 | Policy staff who will lead rooftop activation |
| **Technical** | 1, 6, 7 | Data and GIS teams who will prepare data and run the tools |
| **Complete** | 1 to 8 | Mixed teams starting a rooftop activation programme |

## Choosing formats

| Format | Best for |
|---|---|
| **Presentations** (online or in person, ideally recorded) | Introducing concepts and shared definitions to large groups (modules 1 to 6) |
| **Tool demonstrations** | Showing how the tools work and how to read their results (module 7) |
| **Hands-on workshops** in small groups | Applying the approach to the participants' own city (exercises in modules 2 to 8) |
| **Peer exchange** between authorities | Comparing approaches, constraints and solutions across cities |
| **Site visits** | Seeing real rooftop projects and hearing the story behind the decisions |

Combine presentations with hands-on work: the exercises are where participants connect the approach to their own reality. Where participants speak different languages, run presentations in a common language and workshops in the local language.

## Adapting the training to the local context

- **Use local examples.** Replace generic examples with roofs, districts and policies from the participants' city whenever possible.
- **Adapt the definitions.** The urban challenges, owner types and district categories are a starting point. Encourage participants to adjust them to their context.
- **Use pilots as references, not templates.** Experiences from other cities show what worked under certain conditions. Discuss what would be different locally.
- **Don't assume data readiness.** Many authorities do not yet have 3D models or linked datasets. The methodology modules work without them.

## Reflecting and following up

Learning continues after the sessions. At the end of each module, ask participants to note:

- what they learned that is relevant for their work;
- which challenges or decision moments they foresee in their organisation;
- which questions remain open.

Collecting these notes during the training, rather than only at the end, helps turn individual insights into shared knowledge the organisation can act upon. Consider a follow-up session a few months later to discuss progress on the action plans from module 8.
