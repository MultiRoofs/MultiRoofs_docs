## What "Need" means in MultiRoofs

The MultiRoofs methodology starts from the city's needs. Before deciding what to place on a rooftop, a public authority first clarifies which problems it wants to solve. These problems are the **urban challenges**: the pressures that multifunctional rooftops can help to reduce or adapt to. In the MultiRoofs framework, they represent the demand side against which the potential of rooftops (the "Can") and the willingness of owners (the "Want") are assessed.

## Where the challenges come from

The list of urban challenges builds on a framework first created for the Roofscape project in 2022 by MVRDV and the City of Rotterdam. MultiRoofs extended it after consulting three reference frameworks:

- the [UN Sustainable Development Goals](https://sdgs.un.org/goals),
- the [European Green Deal](https://commission.europa.eu/strategy-and-policy/priorities-2019-2024/european-green-deal_en),
- the Rainbow Rooftop Research of the European Creative Rooftop Network.

The result is a taxonomy of **10 urban challenges** divided into **47 sub-challenges**.

## The ten urban challenges at a glance

The challenges fall into three broad dimensions.

**Cities and natural systems.** Climate adaptation and the protection of nature are closely linked. Cities must cope with heat stress, flooding and water scarcity by improving water management and preparing for extreme weather. At the same time, ecological networks are fragmented, so green and blue spaces need to be reconnected and biodiversity protected. Healthier urban ecosystems also make cities more resilient to climate change. Challenges associated to this dimension are the following:

1. Climate adaptation and resilience: Rising temperatures, heavier rainfall and longer dry spells hit cities hard because their sealed, built-up surfaces trap heat and cannot absorb water.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Urban heat islands | Reduce heat stress caused by sealed surfaces, limited greenery, dense built form and other urban heat sources. |
| Flood risk | Limit damage and disruption caused by extremely high water levels in seas, lakes, rivers and canals due to heavy rainfall and sea-level rise (fluvial flooding). |
| Stormwater management | Manage runoff and provide enough buffer capacity during extreme weather to prevent local flooding (pluvial flooding). |
| Reuse rainwater | Capture rainwater and reuse it, for example for toilets, laundry and irrigation, to reduce pressure on the municipal water supply. |
| Improve water quality | Prevent pollution of surface water and groundwater from street runoff and wastewater. |
| Drought resistance | Strengthen resilience to long dry periods by securing water resources and using drought-resistant vegetation (trees, shrubs, grasses and crops). |
| Lack of water | Ensure reliable access to sufficient clean water, including drinking water, for all urban uses. |

2. Loss of nature and biodiversity: Urban growth and infrastructure break natural habitats into isolated patches, leaving little room for plants and animals to live and move.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Decline of ecological (green and blue) corridors | Reconnect green spaces so that wildlife can move through the city. |
| Habitat fragmentation | Reduce the division of natural habitats caused by urban expansion and infrastructure. |
| Abundance of species | Protect and increase the diversity and population of native species in urban ecosystems. |
| Food security vs biodiversity | Balance urban food production with the restoration of urban biodiversity. |

**The low-carbon and resource-efficient transition.** Reducing emissions requires more energy-efficient buildings, more renewable energy, and solutions for storage and grid capacity. Ageing infrastructure often cannot meet current demand, let alone the growing needs of electrification and sustainable mobility. Circular economy principles complement this transition by reducing resource use and waste through reuse, recycling and better material flows. Challenges associated to this dimension are the following:

3. Decarbonizing energy systems: Buildings consume a large share of a city’s energy, and replacing fossil fuels with renewables requires space, storage and upgrades that many existing buildings lack.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Energy-efficient buildings | Reduce energy demand by improving building performance. |
| Renewable energy adoption | Increase the integration of renewable sources such as solar and wind. |
| Heat / lack of heat sources | Generate heat from renewable sources, such as ground-coupled heat exchangers, thermal energy storage, residual heat and aquathermal energy. |
| Lack of storage capacity | Address the limited capacity to store renewable energy and balance fluctuating supply and demand. |
| Transformation (heritage) restrictions | Overcome regulatory and heritage constraints that limit energy-efficient or renewable technologies in historic buildings and districts. |

4. Aging, unfit, or fragile infrastructure: Many water, energy and transport networks were designed for past needs and struggle to cope with today’s demand, electrification and climate pressures.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Outdated water, energy, and transport systems | Address ageing infrastructure that is not efficient enough for current or future use. |
| Lack of (energy) network capacity | Increase the capacity of energy networks (electricity, hydrogen and heat) to meet demand. |
| Electric grid for EV charging | Strengthen grid capacity and infrastructure for the growing demand for electric vehicle charging. |
| Protection of cultural heritage blocks climate transition | Balance the preservation of historic buildings with the need for upgrades and climate adaptation. |

5. Transition to circular economy: Cities consume large amounts of materials and water but lack the space and systems needed to collect, repair, reuse and recycle them.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Water overflow | Promote water reuse and strengthen sustainable urban water cycles. |
| Low recycling rates | Raise awareness and expand infrastructure to improve recycling performance. |
| Material reuse gaps | Create stronger systems and platforms for material recovery, repurposing and circular use, such as space for material storage, customised services and repair. |
| Trash collection systems | Improve waste separation and collection infrastructure to enable higher recycling and reuse rates. |

**Social and institutional conditions.** Mobility, housing, health and equality determine whether the transition benefits everyone. Sustainable mobility reduces car dependency and frees public space. Affordable housing addresses unequal access to decent living conditions. Health and well-being depend on environmental quality, access to services and social ties, which are unevenly distributed across the population. Governance ties all of this together by coordinating responsibilities, setting rules, securing funding and enabling data-driven decisions. Challenges associated to this dimension are the following:

6. Sustainable and equitable mobility: Car traffic, parking and deliveries occupy much of the limited street space, causing congestion and leaving little room for walking, cycling and public life.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Traffic congestion | Reduce car dependency and traffic pressure, for example by promoting walking, cycling and public transport. |
| Poor slow mobility infrastructure | Expand accessible networks for walking, cycling and other forms of active mobility. |
| Parking demand in public space | Reduce parking needs in public space so that the space can be reclaimed for people and other uses. |
| Lack of shared mobility services and infrastructure | Introduce sharing systems (bikes, scooters, cars and driverless vehicles) and shared parking hubs. |
| Space occupancy for logistics/delivery services | Optimise the use of limited street space to accommodate growing urban logistics and deliveries. |

7. Affordable housing: Growing urban populations and rising prices make adequate housing increasingly hard to find, while land for new construction is scarce.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Housing shortage | Increase housing supply to meet growing demand in urban areas. |
| Housing mismatch | Align housing types, sizes and locations with the needs of different households. |
| Affordability crisis | Address rising housing prices and improve access to adequate housing. |
| Social housing | Provide enough affordable and social housing for vulnerable and low-income groups. |
| Transformation (heritage) restriction | Balance the preservation of historic buildings with the need for additional housing. |

8. Urban health and well-being: Pollution, noise, a lack of green and sports areas, and social isolation affect the physical and mental health of city residents.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Poor air quality | Reduce exposure to pollution and improve clean air standards. |
| Noise pollution | Minimise noise in sensitive areas such as housing, hospitals and schools. |
| Lack of green space, sport facilities | Expand accessible green areas and facilities that encourage active and healthy lifestyles. |
| Lack of (public) facilities | Provide accessible public services and facilities that meet community needs, such as schools, childcare, libraries and community centres. |
| Mental health problems | Support mental health and reduce stress-related conditions in the urban environment. |
| Loneliness and happiness | Foster social connections and community well-being. |

9. Social inequality and segregation: Access to good services, safe public spaces and healthy environments is unevenly spread, which deepens divisions between neighbourhoods and communities.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Neighbourhood segregation | Break down social and spatial divisions between neighbourhoods and communities. |
| Unequal access to services/facilities | Address differences in the availability and quality of essential services, such as schools, childcare, libraries and community centres. |
| Lack of third spaces, social safety/security | Expand safe and inclusive gathering places. |

10. Governance: Responsibilities are spread across departments and levels of government, and gaps in rules, funding and data make coordinated action difficult.

| Sub-challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| Split of competences | Bridge fragmented responsibilities between authorities. |
| No regulation framework to enforce strategies | Establish and update regulations, and support their enforcement. |
| Lack of funding | Secure sustainable financial resources and policy support. |
| No framework for data and monitoring | Create systems to collect, share and monitor data. |

```{note}
Heritage appears in three challenges. In **Decarbonizing energy systems**, it limits energy upgrades. In **Affordable housing**, it limits extra housing such as rooftop extensions. In **Aging, unfit, or fragile infrastructure**, it slows climate adaptation of historic buildings in general.
```

## Adapting the challenges to your city

The ten challenges are a general guide to what a city may need to mitigate or adapt to. **They are not a fixed checklist.** Each city has its own urgencies and political agenda, so the meaning and weight of each challenge varies from place to place. Before using the taxonomy, clarify and adapt the definitions so that they are understandable and relevant for the people who will use them in your organisation.

Priorities can also differ within a city. A dense city centre may face heat stress and a lack of green space, while an industrial district may struggle more with water and energy. For this reason, MultiRoofs looks at challenges at both city and district level.

## Identifying your needs: roadmap steps 1 to 3

The first three steps of the [8-step rooftop activation roadmap](../about-multiroofs/index.md) cover the "Need".

**Step 1. Set city-wide priorities.** Select and rank the challenges that matter most for the city as a whole. Combine several sources of evidence: open data, existing policy, expert knowledge, political direction and public opinion.

**Step 2. Translate priorities to districts.** Where relevant, determine which challenges apply to each urban district. District priorities should be consistent with the city-wide priorities so that local action contributes to the broader goals.

**Step 3. Turn challenges into measurable targets.** When data is available, link each challenge to indicators and quantified goals. Many cities already set such goals in their policy documents, for example kWh of renewable energy, m³ of water retention, the number of homes added through rooftop extensions, or the number of plant and animal species. These targets can then be translated into the rooftop area needed for each function.

The [Urban Challenges assessment tool](../tools/challenge_assessment/index.md) supports these steps.

- MultiRoofs starts from what the city needs, not from what roofs can hold.
- Urban challenges are organised in 10 categories and 47 sub-challenges.
- The taxonomy is a starting point. Each city adapts the definitions and priorities   to its own context, and priorities can differ between districts.
- Where possible, challenges are quantified into targets so that rooftop interventions can be measured against them.
