# Building characteristics

```{admonition} Learning objectives
:class: tip
After reading this page, you will be able to:

## Why building characteristics matter

Not every function fits on every roof. Buildings differ in their use, age, materials, location and structure, and each of these influences whether a rooftop function is feasible and how it should be designed. The main characteristics are:

| Characteristic | Values |
|---|---|
| Primary use | Residential, commercial, office, public and institutional, cultural and leisure, religious, industrial |
| Construction era | Before 1830, 1830–1918, 1919–1945, 1946–1975, 1976–2000, after 2000 |
| Roof type | Flat, pitched, or a combination of both |
| Building height | Low-rise, mid-rise, high-rise, very high-rise |
| Materials | Brick, stone, concrete, steel, glass, wood |
| Construction method | Traditional, prefabricated |
| Location | Urban, suburban, peri-urban |

Regulations add further constraints:

- **Height limits** are usually stricter in residential and peri-urban areas than in dense urban or business districts.
- **Fire safety** rules may require non-combustible materials and safe access for emergency services.
- **Wind load** is critical for high-rise or exposed buildings and affects the type and stability of rooftop installations.
- **Access** through hatches, ladders or staircases is needed for maintenance, everyday use and emergencies.

## Building types by primary use

**Residential buildings.** Single-family houses and apartment blocks are the most common buildings in urban and suburban areas. They offer many opportunities for rooftop uses that improve residents' quality of life. Apartment buildings managed by homeowner associations require collective decisions.

Rooftop sky gardens of the Penthouse at Forbes Quay in the Grand Canal Dock area of Dublin, Ireland
```

**Commercial buildings.** Shops, shopping centres and hotels are concentrated in
dense or mixed-use areas and often have large flat roofs. Their potential varies
widely, for example between a large shopping centre and a small retail unit.

```{figure} ./images/Beaugrenelle-shopping-centre.webp
:alt: Beaugrenelle shopping centre in Paris
:width: 80%
:align: center
:name: fig-comercial-building

7,000 m² green roof spanning across the Beaugrenelle mall structures is one of the largest ecological rooftops in Paris, France.
```

**Office buildings.** Offices are common in North-West European cities and almost
always have large, accessible flat roofs suitable for many uses. For this reason,
they are treated as a separate category from commercial buildings.

```{figure} ./images/brucity.png
:alt: BruCity (City of Brussels administrative building)
:width: 80%
:align: center
:name: fig-office-building

Rooftop 58 operates at the top of BruCity (City of Brussels administrative building), with a bar and 360° view of the capital.
```

**Public and institutional buildings.** Schools, hospitals and government buildings are spread across the city. They often combine large roof surfaces with a public function, which makes them good candidates for visible demonstration projects.

```{figure} ./images/Erasmus-MC-rotterdam.jpg
:alt: Hospital Erasmus MC, Rotterdam
:width: 80%
:align: center
:name: fig-public-building

The Erasmus MC hospital building in Rotterdam is built with two rooftop gardens accessible to patients, visitors and staff, totalling almost 3,000 m², described as the largest in the Netherlands at that height, including 38 fruit trees. The roof insulates the building, reduces heat stress for patients and staff, lowers cooling and heating costs, and retains water to limit flooding.
```

**Cultural and leisure buildings.** Theatres, cinemas and similar venues are fewer in number and mainly located in central districts.

```{figure} ./images/Casestudies-Technopolis-Mechelen.jpg
:alt: Technopolis, Mechelen
:width: 80%
:align: center
:name: fig-cultural-building

IZEN installed 1,300 solar panels on the roof of the Flemish science centre Technopolis in Mechelen, producing one third of the centre’s electricity, as the high point of a sustainability programme started in 2007. Since Technopolis exists to explain science and technology to the public and schools, it fits the demonstration logic well.
```

**Religious buildings.** Places of worship are relatively rare and often protected as heritage, which can limit rooftop interventions.

```{figure} ./images/ballymun-dublin.jpg
:alt: St Pappin’s Parish in Ballymun, Dublin
:width: 80%
:align: center
:name: fig-religious-building

St Pappin’s Parish in Ballymun, which runs three churches, first installed solar on its pastoral centre, then on the roof of St Joseph’s church, with the other two churches next. A parishioner who works for a solar company framed it as a way to keep churches open as they struggle with costs.
```

**Industrial buildings.** Factories and warehouses are located in suburban or industrial zones. Their roof structures are sometimes lightweight and need a structural assessment, but their very large roof areas can have a significant impact on their surroundings.

```{figure} ./images/pringles.avif
:alt: MG Real Estate distribution centre supplying the Pringles factory
:width: 80%
:align: center
:name: fig-industrial-building

Kellanova, the parent company of Pringles (formerly Kellogg’s), commissioned 13,000 solar panels on MG Real Estate’s distribution centre. An underground cable carries the power under a railway line directly to the Pringles factory about 700 metres away. The panels produce 7,000 MWh per year, the equivalent of 2,000 households and 15% of the factory’s electricity needs.
```

## Construction eras

The age of a building reflects the architectural styles, materials and structural capacity of its time.

| Era | Typical features | Implications for rooftops |
|---|---|---|
| **Before 1830** | Pre-industrial buildings with load-bearing masonry, timber and pitched roofs | Often protected as heritage, which limits interventions |
| **1830–1918** | Rapid industrial-era urban growth, often with modest materials; pitched roofs dominate | May have structural or access limitations |
| **1919–1945** | Wider use of reinforced concrete and steel, lighter structures and larger spans; first flat roofs | Flat roofs greatly increase opportunities for rooftop use |
| **1946–1975** | Standardised, large-scale and often prefabricated post-war construction | Large flat roofs, but structural assessment is often needed due to ageing materials, limited load capacity and poor thermal performance |
| **1976–2000** | More diverse designs; first thermal requirements after the 1970s oil crises | Generally better thermal performance than earlier eras |
| **After 2000** | Energy performance and sustainability requirements | More often designed with rooftop uses such as solar panels or green roofs in mind |

Classifying buildings by era helps estimate roof form, accessibility and structural capacity across large parts of a city, even when detailed building data is missing.

```{admonition} Key takeaways
:class: important
- A building's use, age, roof type, height, materials and location determine which
  rooftop functions are feasible.
- Regulations on height, fire safety, wind load and access set further limits.
- Construction era is a useful proxy for structural capacity and roof form.
- Flat roofs became common from the interwar period, but post-war buildings often
  need a structural check before adding heavy functions.
```
