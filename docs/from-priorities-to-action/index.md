The previous pages describe the three perspectives of the MultiRoofs methodology separately: what the city [needs](../urban-challenges/index.md), what its [rooftops and buildings can offer](../rooftops-and-buildings/index.md), and what [owners want](../owners-and-incentives/index.md). This page explains how they come together to help a public authority decide where to start, which rooftop solutions to promote and how to convince owners to act.

## The MultiRoofs framework

The MultiRoofs framework connects two sides:

- **What can be done:** the technical capacity of each roof and the preferences of the city, summarised in a **rooftop passport** for each roof.
- **What needs to be done:** the urban challenges the city wants to address, organised in a **local menu of urban priorities**.

Matching the two shows where rooftop activation would be both feasible and useful. This match is then confronted with **what stakeholders want**, since no roof is activated without the agreement of its owner.

## The 8-step roadmap at a glance

The [roadmap](../about-multiroofs/index.md) turns the framework into a sequence of steps. It structures decisions, but does not make them: each step requires the judgement of the public authority. The following is a summary of the each steps we have discussed so far.

| Step | Phase | Question | Explained in |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Set city-wide priorities | Need (Moeten) | Which challenges matter most for the city? | [Urban challenges](../urban-challenges/index.md) |
| 2. Translate priorities to districts | Need (Moeten) | Which challenges apply to each district? | [Urban challenges](../urban-challenges/index.md) |
| 3. Turn challenges into measurable targets | Need (Moeten) | How much needs to be achieved? | [Urban challenges](../urban-challenges/index.md) |
| 4. Map owners and buildings | Can (Kunnen) | What buildings and owners are there? | [Rooftops and buildings](../rooftops-and-buildings/index.md) |
| 5. Build a rooftop passport | Can (Kunnen) | What can each roof offer? | [Rooftops and buildings](../rooftops-and-buildings/index.md) |
| 6. Match opportunities with challenges | Want (Willen) | Where do opportunities meet the challenges? | [Owners and incentives](../owners-and-incentives/index.md) |
| 7. Select function combinations | Pilots | Which combinations of functions best serve the priorities? | [This page](#step-7-select-function-combinations) |
| 8. Design targeted incentives | Pilots | Which instruments will convince owners to act? | [Owners and incentives](../owners-and-incentives/index.md) |

Cities do not need to follow every step in full or in strict order. The pilot cities enter the roadmap at different points, apply it with different levels of
data, and move at different speeds.

### Step 7. Select function combinations

Once the priorities are clear for each district, building type and owner type, the city looks for combinations of rooftop functions that reinforce each other and
respond to those priorities. Green and blue roofs together, for example, serve water retention, while yellow and purple roofs together support sustainable
housing. See [golden combinations](../rooftops-and-buildings/rooftop-functions.md) for more examples.

## Strategic prioritisation

Deciding where and how to act requires more than mapping. The MultiRoofs partner cities developed a strategic prioritisation framework with three pillars. Used together, they help a public authority move from ambition to a realistic plan.

### Pillar 1: Clarify priorities

Every rooftop programme starts by clarifying which challenges matter most in a given location. The partner cities scored the relevance of the ten urban challenges for
each district type in their city, and estimated how strongly each owner type might care about the same challenges.

Two lessons emerged:

- **Urgency varies by district.** Most cities recognise climate adaptation and energy decarbonisation as important, but their urgency differs between districts. Dense urban districts tend to struggle most with heat and a lack of green space, industrial districts with water and energy, and residential neighbourhoods with a mix of environmental, mobility and social needs.
- **City and owner priorities often diverge.** Planners may see heat reduction or biodiversity as essential in a district, while owners care more about energy savings, property value or low maintenance. A rooftop proposal that does not match the motivations of the people who approve and pay for it is likely to fail.

Scoring district needs and owner priorities on the same scale makes these gaps visible and shows where incentives are needed. The scores themselves are indicative, since they reflect expert judgement rather than formal decisions. Their main value is that they make implicit assumptions explicit and give colleagues a shared basis for discussion.

### Pillar 2: Compare costs and benefits

Rooftop options are often assessed on their own, which gives an incomplete picture. MultiRoofs compares each rooftop option with conventional ground-level alternatives for the same challenge, such as parks, stormwater basins, ground-mounted solar panels or community facilities in public space. This shows when a rooftop is the better choice, and when it is the only option, for example in dense areas with no free space at ground level.

Two findings stand out:

- **Combinations pay off.** Multifunctional rooftops generate synergies that single interventions cannot achieve. Green and blue roofs for water retention, green and yellow for energy efficiency, and green and red for urban farming and social use were repeatedly identified as worth more than the sum of their parts.
- **Non-financial constraints are costs too.** Structural limits, heritage restrictions, complicated ownership, administrative burden, maintenance needs and conflicts between functions (such as public access versus safety) must be counted from the start. If they are overlooked, they can undermine a project even when its benefits are attractive.

Reliable financial data on combined rooftop functions is still scarce, because their value depends heavily on local regulations and technical conditions. The pilots are therefore collecting evidence to refine these comparisons.

### Pillar 3: Design incentives that work

Even with clear priorities and favourable comparisons, activation depends on owners' willingness to act. Financial incentives alone rarely achieve this: they work best in combination with advisory services, clear communication, accessible tools and stable rules. See [Incentives](../owners-and-incentives/incentives.md) for the types of instruments and the lessons from the partner cities.

## Cross-cutting principles

Three principles apply across all steps and pillars:

1. **Alignment is essential.** Rooftop activation requires city needs, building and district capacities, and owner motivations to line up. When they don't, activation slows down or becomes fragmented. Making decisions explicit helps to see where incentives must bridge the gaps.
2. **Transparent comparison reduces conflict.** Shared indicators, scoring systems and comparable information make it easier to explain choices and build legitimacy with colleagues, owners and residents.
3. **Incentives must be integrated and reliable.** Cities that combine financial, advisory, digital and regulatory measures in stable, multi-year programmes are more likely to unlock private investment.

## Testing the approach in the pilots (TBC)

The six pilot cities in MultiRoofs (Rotterdam, Brussels, Mechelen, Mannheim, Île-de-France and Dublin) applied this approach to real districts, buildings and owners. The pilots tested
how the tools developed in MultiRoofs performs with local data, how authorities can identify the right owners at the right moment, and which forms of support lead to action. Their
results will refine the roadmap and the guidance on this site.
